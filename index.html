The opening move

Three shores.
One western front.

Operation Torch was the Allied invasion of French North Africa during the Second World War. The operation began on 8 November 1942 and involved American and British forces.

The landings took place at Casablanca, Oran and Algiers, opening a major Allied campaign in North Africa.

“Torch” was a cover name.

The operation’s code name helped conceal the invasion’s real destination from the Axis powers.

More than 100,000 Allied troops came ashore.

The force mixed American ground troops with British naval and air support.

It was the first major Anglo-American operation in the European-North African theatre.

The landings created a new western front in the Mediterranean.

The Allies hoped to avoid a long fight with France.

Admiral François Darlan’s intervention helped bring the fighting to an end.

Algiers became the political hinge.

Its capture gave the Allies an important headquarters and a route toward Tunisia.

Convoys crossed the Atlantic under threat.

Amphibious ships and escorts had to reach North Africa while German U-boats hunted Allied shipping.

The first landings began before dawn on 8 November 1942.

The early-hour attacks caught many defenders by surprise.

Airfields mattered as much as beaches.

Capturing bases allowed Allied aircraft to support the advance and reach farther into the Mediterranean.

Torch pressured Germany from the south.

The campaign helped force Axis troops into a two-front struggle in the Mediterranean.

By 11 November, organized resistance had largely ended.

Fighting stopped following agreements between Allied and French authorities.

Torch pointed directly toward Tunisia.

The landings secured a base for the next phase of the North African campaign.

It was a rehearsal for larger landings.

The lessons of Torch shaped later Allied operations, including Sicily and Normandy.

The North African campaign ended with the Axis surrender in Tunisia.

That surrender came in May 1943, six months after the Torch landings.
